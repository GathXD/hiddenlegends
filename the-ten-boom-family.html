COURAGE & RESCUE

The ten
Boom Family

The Dutch family who risked their lives to hide Jewish people during the Holocaust.

THE HIDDEN STORY

Family: The ten Boom Family

Home: Haarlem, Netherlands

Field: Christian Resistance & Humanitarian Aid

Known For: Hiding Jewish people during the Holocaust

In the Dutch city of Haarlem, above a small watch shop, a Christian family quietly made a decision that would put their lives in danger. The ten Boom family did not begin the Second World War as resistance fighters. They were watchmakers, ordinary citizens who lived together and shared a strong Christian faith. But when Nazi Germany occupied the Netherlands, their understanding of faith and responsibility led them into a dangerous mission: hiding Jewish people from persecution and helping them escape.

The family was led by Casper ten Boom, an elderly watchmaker known for his kindness and generosity. He lived with his daughters, Betsie and Corrie, in the family home at 19 Barteljorisstraat. The building became both their workplace and, eventually, a hiding place. Corrie was deeply involved in the watch business, while Betsie helped manage the household and shop.

Before the war, the ten Booms were already known for helping people in need. Their Christian faith taught them that every person had value. When Nazi persecution of Jewish people became increasingly severe, they could not simply ignore what was happening around them.

Jewish families in the Netherlands faced restrictions, arrests, deportations, and the constant threat of being separated from their loved ones. Some people desperately needed somewhere to hide. The ten Booms began opening their home to those who had nowhere else to go.

Corrie became increasingly involved in the Dutch resistance. She helped organize a network that provided hiding places, ration cards, identification papers, and other assistance to people being hunted by the Nazis. The family home became part of this network.

A secret room was constructed behind a false wall in Corrie's bedroom. It was small and difficult to reach, but it could conceal people during Nazi searches. When danger approached, those hiding in the house could disappear into the room and remain completely silent until the search was over.

The ten Booms understood that hiding people was extremely dangerous. If the Nazis discovered what they were doing, everyone in the house could be arrested. Even neighbors could become suspicious. The family therefore had to live with the possibility that an ordinary knock on the door could become a matter of life and death.

The people who came to the house were often frightened and exhausted. Some had already lost relatives or homes. Others were afraid that they would be discovered at any moment. The ten Booms gave them food, shelter, and somewhere they could briefly feel safe.

Their resistance work depended on cooperation with many other people. Corrie helped connect those needing protection with members of a wider network. Some people were hidden in private homes, while others were moved to farms or other safe locations. False documents and ration cards were also important because people in hiding could not safely obtain supplies through ordinary channels.

The family knew that not everyone could be saved. Every new person brought another risk. More people meant more food was needed and more chances that someone might notice something unusual. Nevertheless, they continued helping.

In February 1944, the danger finally reached the ten Boom family. A Dutch informant betrayed their resistance activities, and the Gestapo raided the house. Corrie, Betsie, Casper, and other family members and associates were arrested.

The secret room, however, remained undiscovered during the raid. Several Jewish people and resistance workers were hiding there. They stayed silent for hours while the Gestapo searched the building. Eventually, members of the resistance were able to rescue the people who were still hidden inside. They survived the raid.

The ten Booms were not so fortunate. Casper ten Boom, already in his eighties, was imprisoned. He died only days after his arrest. Corrie and Betsie were eventually sent to the Ravensbrück concentration camp in Germany.

Life at Ravensbrück was brutal. Corrie and Betsie were forced to live in terrible conditions, surrounded by hunger, disease, exhaustion, and violence. Betsie nevertheless continued encouraging her sister to care for others and to hold onto their faith.

The sisters eventually found ways to create a small community among the prisoners. They prayed together and shared whatever food or encouragement they could. Even inside the concentration camp, Betsie insisted that hatred should not be allowed to destroy the people they were trying to remain.

Betsie's health eventually deteriorated severely. She died at Ravensbrück in December 1944. Corrie survived and was released later that month because of an administrative error. Other prisoners who were part of the same age group were not as fortunate.

After the war, Corrie discovered that her release had happened shortly before the women in her camp section were killed. She returned to the Netherlands carrying the memories of her family, the people they had helped, and the terrible suffering she had witnessed.

Corrie eventually began speaking publicly about her experiences. She traveled internationally and told the story of her family and their decision to help people who were being persecuted. Her story became widely known through her autobiography, The Hiding Place, which described the family's resistance work and their imprisonment.

One of the most difficult parts of Corrie's later life was dealing with the question of forgiveness. She had experienced the death of family members and witnessed terrible cruelty. Yet she continued to speak about forgiveness and the possibility of choosing love rather than allowing hatred to control a person's life.

The ten Boom family is remembered not because they possessed political power or military strength, but because they used what they had. They had a home, a shop, a network of friends, and a willingness to help. Those ordinary resources became tools of resistance when people needed protection.

Their story also demonstrates that courage does not always mean being fearless. The ten Booms understood that they could be arrested or killed. They knew the consequences of discovery. Courage meant acting despite that fear because they believed that protecting innocent people was worth the risk.

During the Holocaust, countless individuals chose to look away because helping someone could place their own lives in danger. The ten Boom family chose a different path. They opened their doors to people who were being hunted and became part of a network that helped many survive.

The family home in Haarlem is now remembered as the Corrie ten Boom House. The building preserves the memory of the family, the watch shop, and the hidden room where people once waited in silence for the danger to pass.

The legacy of the ten Boom family is ultimately a story about the value of one human life. They could not stop the war or dismantle the Nazi regime. They could not save everyone. But they could protect the people who stood in front of them, one person at a time.

Corrie, Betsie, and Casper ten Boom remind us that history is not shaped only by presidents, generals, and armies. Sometimes it is shaped by families who decide that doing what is right matters more than protecting themselves. Their story continues to challenge people to choose courage when silence would be easier, compassion when indifference would be safer, and forgiveness when hatred seems justified.
