SCIENCE

Percy
Julian

The chemist who made important medicines affordable through plant-based chemistry.

THE HIDDEN STORY

Born: 1899

Died: 1975

Field: Chemistry

Known For: Plant-based drug synthesis and industrial chemistry

Percy Lavon Julian was born on April 11, 1899, in Montgomery, Alabama, at a time when opportunities for Black Americans were severely restricted by segregation. His parents, James Sumner Julian and Elizabeth Lena Adams Julian, placed enormous importance on education. His father worked as a railroad mail clerk, while his mother was a schoolteacher. Julian's grandparents had formerly been enslaved, making his later achievements even more remarkable in the context of the barriers surrounding his generation.

Julian showed an extraordinary ability in school, but his path toward higher education was not straightforward. Black students in Montgomery had extremely limited access to advanced schooling. After completing his early education, Julian moved to Birmingham, Alabama, where he attended a school for Black students. Although his preparation was considered inadequate for a traditional college education, he refused to let that determine his future.

In 1916, Julian entered DePauw University in Indiana as a probationary student. He had to take additional high-school-level courses while beginning college work, but he quickly demonstrated that he belonged there. He excelled in chemistry and became a member of Phi Beta Kappa. In 1920, he graduated as the valedictorian of his class.

After graduating, Julian taught chemistry at Fisk University before earning a fellowship to study at Harvard University. He completed a master's degree in organic chemistry in 1923. Yet racial discrimination continued to restrict his opportunities. Harvard would not allow him to pursue the doctorate he wanted in his field, and major universities were unwilling to give a Black scientist the academic positions he deserved.

Julian continued teaching and conducting research at historically Black colleges, including Howard University and West Virginia State College. In 1929, a fellowship allowed him to travel to the University of Vienna in Austria. There he studied organic chemistry and the chemistry of plants under the distinguished chemist Ernst Späth.

Julian earned his PhD in 1931 and returned to the United States determined to continue scientific research. His time in Vienna had deepened his interest in natural compounds found in plants, an area of chemistry that would eventually lead to some of his most important discoveries.

Julian returned to DePauw University as a research fellow, where he worked with his colleague Josef Pikl. Together they began investigating physostigmine, a powerful compound found naturally in the Calabar bean. Physicians already knew that physostigmine could be used to treat glaucoma, but obtaining the compound in large quantities was difficult because it had to be extracted from its natural source.

Julian and Pikl attempted to reproduce the complicated molecule through chemical synthesis. Their work was extremely demanding. They had to determine the structure of the compound and then construct it through a carefully planned sequence of chemical reactions.

Their research eventually led to the first successful total synthesis of physostigmine in 1935. The achievement established Julian as an important chemist and demonstrated that a useful medicinal substance found in a plant could be produced through laboratory chemistry. This made it possible to obtain the compound in a more practical and reliable way for medical use.

The breakthrough was particularly significant because Julian had been working in a scientific world where racial discrimination remained widespread. Despite the importance of his research, DePauw did not offer him the permanent academic position he deserved. Instead of allowing this rejection to end his career, Julian moved into industrial research.

In 1936, he joined the Glidden Company in Chicago as a research chemist and later became director of its Soya Products Division. There he discovered that soybean compounds could be used for far more than food. He began developing practical chemical products from soybean proteins and oils.

During World War II, Julian developed Aero-Foam, a soybean-based fire-extinguishing foam designed to put out gasoline and oil fires. The material was used by the U.S. Navy and became an important example of how Julian's research could solve practical problems and protect lives.

His research into soybeans also opened another important path. Julian discovered ways to isolate plant sterols and use them as starting materials for the synthesis of hormones. His work helped make progesterone and other steroid hormones available on a much larger scale than before.

Julian later developed a practical process for producing synthetic cortisone from plant-derived materials. Cortisone became an important treatment for inflammatory diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis. Before large-scale synthetic production, cortisone was extremely expensive and difficult to obtain. Julian's work helped make steroid medicines more accessible.

Julian's success in industrial chemistry eventually allowed him to establish his own company, Julian Laboratories. Beginning in the 1950s, he continued developing methods for producing pharmaceutical chemicals from plant materials, including steroids derived from Mexican yams. His company became an important center for chemical manufacturing and research.

In 1961, Julian sold Julian Laboratories to the pharmaceutical company Smith, Kline & French for $2.3 million. The sale made him one of the first African American millionaires. But financial success was only one part of what made his career remarkable. Throughout his life, Julian continued to confront discrimination and worked to create opportunities for other Black scientists.

His own experiences had shown him how easily talent could be overlooked when prejudice was allowed to control institutions. Julian therefore became involved in civil rights and supported efforts to challenge racial discrimination. His scientific career and his public work were connected by the same belief: ability should not be limited by race.

His achievements eventually received major recognition. He was awarded the Spingarn Medal by the NAACP in 1947 and received numerous honorary degrees and scientific honors. In 1973, he was elected to the National Academy of Sciences, becoming only the second African American elected to that organization and the first African American chemist to receive that distinction.

Julian continued working and supporting scientific research until late in his life. He died of liver cancer on April 19, 1975, in Waukegan, Illinois, eight days after his seventy-sixth birthday. By then, his work had already influenced medicine, industrial chemistry, and the lives of people suffering from serious diseases.

What makes Percy Julian a Hidden Legend is the extraordinary distance between the obstacles he faced and the impact he eventually achieved. He entered college without the educational preparation that many of his classmates had received. He faced universities that refused him opportunities because of his race. Yet he continued studying, researching, experimenting, and creating.

His discoveries reached far beyond a single laboratory. His synthesis of physostigmine helped make an important glaucoma treatment more available. His work with plant sterols contributed to the production of hormones. His research on synthetic cortisone helped lower the barriers to treatment for people suffering from inflammatory diseases.

His achievements also showed the power of looking at familiar natural materials differently. A soybean might appear to be an ordinary crop, but Julian saw chemical possibilities hidden inside it. Through careful experimentation, he transformed plant compounds into materials that could be used in medicine, manufacturing, and fire protection.

Julian's life was not simply a story of scientific success. It was also a story of persistence. He repeatedly encountered doors that were closed because of racism, yet he continued finding new paths forward. When academic institutions denied him opportunities, he moved into industry. When others underestimated plant chemistry, he explored its possibilities. When discrimination limited his own career, he worked to create opportunities for others.

His legacy can be measured in patents, medicines, scientific discoveries, and institutions, but it can also be measured in the people who were inspired by his example. Julian demonstrated that scientific excellence could challenge assumptions about who was capable of leading important research.

In 1990, the National Inventors Hall of Fame recognized Julian for his work. The American Chemical Society later recognized his synthesis of physostigmine as one of the major achievements in American chemistry. His laboratory and scientific contributions continue to be remembered as important parts of the history of chemistry and medicine.

Percy Julian did not simply discover useful chemicals. He changed what people believed a scientist could accomplish in the face of enormous obstacles. His work connected chemistry with medicine, industry, and human welfare, while his life challenged the racial barriers surrounding American science.

His story reminds us that a scientific breakthrough can begin with something incredibly ordinary. For Julian, that could be a small bean, a soybean, or a plant compound that other people might have overlooked. Through chemistry, he discovered possibilities hidden inside these materials and turned them into solutions for real human problems.

Percy Julian's greatest legacy is therefore larger than any single invention. He proved that determination, imagination, and scientific skill could overcome barriers that seemed permanent. His discoveries helped people see more clearly, live with less pain, and survive dangerous fires, while his career helped open doors for generations of scientists who followed him.

His life is a reminder that history does not always remember the people who made the most noise. Sometimes it remembers the people who quietly solved difficult problems and changed millions of lives through work that began in a laboratory.
