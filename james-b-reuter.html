CHRISTIAN

James B. Reuter

The Jesuit priest who used radio, television, theater, and education to serve the Philippines and keep a voice on the air during a time of political upheaval.

THE HIDDEN STORY

James B. Reuter

Born: May 21, 1916

Died: December 31, 2012

Field: Priest, Educator & Communicator

Known For: Catholic Media & Radio Veritas

In the history of the Philippines, some of the people who changed the country were not politicians, generals, or presidents. Some worked behind microphones, cameras, classrooms, and theater curtains. One of them was James Bertram Reuter, an American Jesuit priest who spent almost his entire adult life serving in the Philippines. He became a writer, teacher, director, broadcaster, and communicator whose work connected faith, education, theater, and mass media.

James B. Reuter was born on May 21, 1916, in Elizabeth, New Jersey. From a young age, he dreamed of becoming a missionary. After graduating from St. Peter's Preparatory School in Jersey City in 1934, he entered the Society of Jesus and began his Jesuit formation at the novitiate in Wernersville, Pennsylvania.

In 1938, when he was only twenty-two, Reuter was assigned to the Philippines. He arrived in Manila as a young Jesuit scholastic and began a relationship with the country that would last for more than seven decades. He studied philosophy in the Philippines and spent time teaching at Ateneo de Manila University. There he became involved in theater, writing, and radio, discovering that communication could become an important part of his ministry.

Reuter was already involved with radio before the Second World War. At Ateneo de Manila, he helped produce The Commonweal Hour, a Sunday-night radio program sponsored by the Catholic Church. He worked alongside young Filipinos who would later become writers, intellectuals, and national leaders. His early experience showed him the enormous potential of radio to reach people far beyond a classroom or church.

But his work in the Philippines was interrupted by war. When Japan invaded the Philippines in 1941, American Jesuits were among those arrested and placed in internment camps. Reuter was sent to the Los Baños internment camp in Laguna. The experience was difficult, and his health suffered during his imprisonment.

After his liberation, Reuter returned to the United States to recover and continue his preparation for the priesthood. On March 24, 1946, he was ordained a Jesuit priest at Woodstock, Maryland. He then studied radio and television communications at Fordham University. Instead of leaving his communication interests behind, he was preparing himself to use them in a much larger way.

In 1948, Reuter returned to the Philippines. He was assigned to Naga City, where he founded the Cathedral Players, a theater group whose performers included priests and religious sisters. Theater became one of the ways he taught and communicated with people, especially young Filipinos. His work showed that education did not have to happen only through textbooks and lectures. Stories, drama, radio, and performance could also teach.

Reuter later returned to Ateneo de Manila, where his talents in communication continued to grow. He directed plays, trained students, worked in radio, and became involved in television. He believed that mass media could be used not simply for entertainment, but also for education, faith, and service.

His connection with the Family Rosary Crusade became especially important. Reuter worked with the movement associated with Father Patrick Peyton, who promoted family prayer through mass communication. Reuter recognized that radio and television could carry religious messages into homes that might never attend a theater, lecture, or public event. He developed Family Theater productions in the Philippines and brought Catholic programming to radio and television.

His work expanded beyond religious broadcasting. He became involved in Philippine theater and television production and helped train generations of performers and communicators. He also wrote and directed productions and became known for combining professional media work with his religious mission.

In the 1960s, Reuter's responsibilities in Catholic communications grew considerably. He became closely associated with the Catholic Church's media work in the Philippines and served as general manager of Radio Veritas from 1965 to 1967. He later became national director of the media arm of the Catholic Church. He also helped establish organizations that would contribute to the development of Catholic communications in the country.

Then came one of the most difficult periods in Philippine history. In 1972, President Ferdinand Marcos declared Martial Law, and many media organizations were closed or placed under government control. Catholic broadcasting was also affected. Reuter's media operations were padlocked, but he continued working to keep Catholic radio stations operating.

Reuter became involved in efforts to preserve communication through the Catholic broadcasting network. According to the Jesuit Conference of Asia Pacific, he served as a liaison with the military and worked to keep the Catholic stations functioning. He also published Communicator, a weekly tabloid that carried news that was not appearing in the government-controlled press. His work placed him in a difficult position because communicating information during authoritarian rule could bring serious consequences.

Reuter was eventually arrested by the Marcos administration. His work was no longer simply about producing television programs or directing plays. The ability to communicate information had become connected to questions of freedom, truth, and public life.

One of the most important chapters of Reuter's life came during the People Power Revolution of 1986. As huge crowds gathered along EDSA, Radio Veritas became an important source of information. The station broadcast reports about what was happening and helped people communicate during the rapidly changing situation.

When Radio Veritas's main transmitter was attacked and stopped broadcasting, the station's staff had to find another way to remain on the air. Reuter helped keep the broadcasts going by moving the operation to another transmitter. The emergency broadcasts became associated with the name Radyo Bandido. The Ramon Magsaysay Award Foundation later recognized Reuter for his use of writing, theater, broadcasting, and teaching as forces for service in the Philippines.

Reuter's role was not to lead the crowds in the streets. His contribution was behind the scenes: helping maintain a communication network at a moment when information was extremely important. Radio gave people news about what was happening around them and helped connect different parts of the movement.

After the People Power Revolution restored a democratic government in 1986, Reuter continued working in communications. He returned to television with the Family Rosary Crusade and continued speaking through radio and other forms of media. His work remained focused on communication, faith, education, and service.

His contributions were recognized beyond the Catholic Church. In 1989, Reuter received the Ramon Magsaysay Award for his work as a writer, theatrical director, broadcaster, and teacher. The award citation described his use of the performing arts and mass media as a force for good in the Philippines.

Reuter also continued writing and teaching. He influenced students, actors, broadcasters, writers, and other people who worked in Philippine media. At St. Paul University Manila, a professorial chair was later named in his honor. His connection to education remained an important part of the legacy he left behind.

Reuter spent more than seventy years of his life connected to the Philippines. Although he had been born in the United States, he considered his life and mission in the Philippines deeply meaningful. In a later interview, he spoke about his affection for the Filipino people and described his long life in the country as a blessing.

In his later years, Reuter became less physically active but continued to be remembered for his work in communications. He died on December 31, 2012, at Our Lady of Peace Hospital in Parañaque, Philippines, at the age of ninety-six.

James B. Reuter's story is unusual because his legacy cannot be placed into only one category. He was a priest, but he was also a teacher. He was a teacher, but he was also a theater director. He worked in theater, but he also helped build radio and television programs. And when the political situation of the Philippines became dangerous, his work in communication became part of a much larger story about access to information and public life.

His life also shows how influence can happen without holding political office or commanding an army. Reuter spent much of his life teaching people how to communicate, creating programs, directing performances, writing, and using media to reach audiences across the Philippines.

For Reuter, communication was more than a profession. It was a way of serving people. Whether he was standing in a theater, teaching students, speaking through a radio microphone, or helping keep a broadcast alive during the People Power Revolution, he continued using the tools available to him to reach others.

James B. Reuter died at ninety-six, but the networks of people and institutions he helped build continued after him. His story reminds us that history is not shaped only by the people standing in front of the camera. Sometimes, the people behind the microphone, directing the production, teaching the next generation, or keeping the signal alive are the ones whose work quietly reaches thousands.

His life was built around a simple idea: that communication could be used to educate, to serve, and to connect people. In the history of Philippine media and Catholic communications, James B. Reuter became one of the people who helped make that idea a reality.

“As each of you has received a gift, use it to serve one another, as good stewards of God's varied grace.” 1 John 4:10

This fits Reuter particularly well because he used communication, teaching, theater, radio, and television as ways of serving others.
