THE HIDDEN STORY

Abdul Sattar Edhi

Born: February 28, 1928

Died: July 8, 2016

Field: Social Work

Known For: The Edhi Foundation

Abdul Sattar Edhi was born on January 1, 1928, in Bantva, a town in the Kathiawar region of British India, which is now part of Gujarat, India. He grew up in a modest Muslim family where compassion for people in need was treated as a responsibility rather than simply an ideal. His mother had a particularly strong influence on him. When Edhi was still a child, she taught him to care for people who were poor, sick, or vulnerable, lessons that would remain with him for the rest of his life.

When Edhi was around eleven years old, his mother became seriously ill. He helped care for her as her condition worsened, an experience that deeply affected him. She eventually developed paralysis and suffered from diabetes, and Edhi continued looking after her for years. Her illness exposed him to suffering at an early age and helped shape his determination to build a life centered on helping others.

In 1947, the partition of British India created the new countries of India and Pakistan. Edhi and his family moved to Pakistan, settling in Karachi. The massive migration brought enormous suffering. Millions of people were displaced, and many arrived in unfamiliar cities without homes, money, or reliable access to medical care. Edhi witnessed this suffering firsthand and became increasingly convinced that ordinary people had to take responsibility for helping those who had been left behind.

Edhi began his humanitarian work on an extremely small scale. He purchased basic medical supplies and established a small free dispensary in Karachi. He also collected donations from local people and used the money to help those who could not afford treatment. Instead of waiting for a large organization or wealthy institution to solve the problems around him, he began building the organization himself, one service at a time.

Edhi's humanitarian work expanded rapidly as more people joined him. In 1951, he established what became known as the Edhi Foundation, creating an organized network dedicated to helping people regardless of their religion, ethnicity, social position, or financial situation. One of the foundation's most recognizable services became its ambulance network. Edhi believed that emergency medical care should reach people quickly, especially those who had no other way to get to a hospital.

What began with a single ambulance eventually developed into one of the largest private ambulance services in the world. Edhi personally drove ambulances during the early years of the organization, responding to emergencies and transporting injured or sick people. The service became especially important during accidents, fires, natural disasters, and periods of political violence when large numbers of people needed immediate assistance.

But ambulances were only one part of Edhi's work. The foundation created shelters for homeless people, homes for orphans, clinics, maternity services, food programs, and facilities for people with disabilities. It also helped abandoned children and provided assistance to people suffering from mental illness. Edhi's philosophy was simple: if a person needed help, that person deserved help.

One of the foundation's most unusual and important services involved unidentified bodies. In a society where families could sometimes be unable to recover or identify relatives who had died, Edhi's organization collected bodies, attempted to identify them, and arranged burials when necessary. The foundation also established a system for accepting abandoned babies, including cradles where parents could leave infants safely rather than abandoning them in dangerous places.

Edhi deliberately lived a remarkably simple life despite the enormous organization that grew around him. He was known for wearing plain clothing and avoiding the luxurious lifestyle that could have come with his fame. He trusted ordinary donations rather than depending entirely on governments or wealthy patrons. This independence allowed the organization to continue providing aid even when political conditions were unstable.

His work was also shaped by a strong belief in equality. The Edhi Foundation did not ask people whether they were Muslim, Christian, Hindu, Sikh, or another faith before offering assistance. Victims of disasters, abandoned children, prisoners, refugees, and people living on the streets could all receive help. Edhi saw humanitarian service as something that belonged to humanity itself rather than to one particular group.

As the Edhi Foundation expanded, Edhi became one of Pakistan's most recognizable humanitarian figures. Yet he continued to emphasize that the organization belonged to the people who supported it, not to him personally. Volunteers, ambulance drivers, nurses, social workers, and donors all played important roles in keeping the network running. Edhi's greatest achievement was therefore not simply the number of people he personally helped, but the humanitarian system he built so that assistance could continue reaching people every day.

His work eventually received international recognition. Edhi received numerous awards for humanitarian service, including the Ramon Magsaysay Award in 1986 and the Lenin Peace Prize. He was also nominated several times for the Nobel Peace Prize. Despite these honors, he remained more interested in practical service than ceremonies or public attention.

Edhi also experienced opposition and danger. Pakistan went through periods of political instability, violence, and religious tension, and humanitarian workers sometimes had to operate in extremely difficult conditions. Edhi's commitment to helping people regardless of their identity could make his work controversial, but he continued to insist that compassion should not depend on political or religious divisions.

Abdul Sattar Edhi died on July 8, 2016, in Karachi at the age of 88. His funeral was attended by large numbers of people, reflecting the extraordinary influence he had developed across Pakistani society. The organization he created continued operating after his death, carrying forward the services that had become essential to millions of people.

Edhi's story is powerful because he did not begin with great wealth, political authority, or advanced technology. He began with a desire to help people who were suffering and the willingness to act on that desire. A small dispensary became a vast humanitarian network, and a single ambulance became part of a nationwide emergency service.

Abdul Sattar Edhi showed that humanitarianism does not always begin with a grand plan. Sometimes it begins with one person seeing someone in need and deciding not to walk past. His life demonstrated that compassion can become an organized force capable of reaching millions. Long after his death, the ambulances, shelters, clinics, orphanages, and countless acts of service connected to his name continue to carry forward the principle that defined his life: no human being should be abandoned simply because they are poor, alone, sick, or forgotten.
