COURAGE & RESCUE

The Ładoś Group

The Polish diplomats and Jewish activists who used forged passports to help Jews escape Nazi persecution during the Holocaust.

THE HIDDEN STORY

The Ładoś Group

Active: c. 1941–1945

Location: Bern, Switzerland

Field: Rescue & Diplomacy

Known For: Forged Latin American passports

The Ładoś Group was a network of Polish diplomats and Jewish activists who worked in Switzerland during the Holocaust to help Jews threatened by Nazi persecution. The group operated mainly from Bern, where members used diplomatic contacts, false documents, and secret cooperation to create a possible escape route for people trapped under Nazi rule. Their work did not look like a battlefield rescue operation. Instead, it depended on passports, names, stamps, letters, money, and information. These pieces of paper could become a matter of life and death.

The group is associated especially with Aleksander Ładoś, the Polish envoy in Switzerland, and several people who worked around the Polish diplomatic mission. Among them were diplomats Stefan Ryniewicz and Konstanty Rokicki, as well as Jewish activists Abraham Silberschein and Chaim Eiss. They did not all have the same role, but they formed a network in which diplomats and Jewish organizations could cooperate. The goal was to provide Jews with documents showing that they were citizens or protected persons of Latin American countries.

During the Holocaust, possessing a foreign passport or certificate could sometimes change the treatment of a Jewish prisoner. People holding certain foreign documents could be placed in separate detention facilities or temporarily spared from deportation while officials considered their status. The documents did not guarantee safety, and Nazi authorities sometimes ignored or rejected them. Even so, they created opportunities that ordinary victims of persecution did not have.

One of the most important parts of the operation involved Paraguay. Members of the network obtained blank or genuine-looking Paraguayan passports and arranged for them to be filled with the names and details of Jews in occupied Europe. These documents were not produced through a normal legal immigration process. They were part of a clandestine rescue effort intended to give people a connection to a neutral or distant country.

Konstanty Rokicki played a particularly important role in producing the documents. Working with Jewish activist Abraham Silberschein, he filled out passports and related papers in Bern. The operation required careful work because names, dates, identification details, and official-looking information had to be written onto documents that might later be inspected by authorities. The people receiving the papers were scattered across countries occupied or controlled by Nazi Germany, so the information also had to be moved through underground networks.

Abraham Silberschein helped organize the Jewish side of the operation. He was connected with the Relico organization, which worked to help Jewish refugees and victims of Nazi persecution. Chaim Eiss, another Jewish activist associated with the network, also helped obtain information and coordinate assistance. Their contacts extended beyond Switzerland and allowed names of people in danger to reach the diplomats who could prepare documents.

The people receiving these papers were not necessarily trying to travel directly to Paraguay. In many cases, the documents were intended to give them a different legal status in the eyes of the authorities. Some people with Latin American papers were sent to internment camps rather than immediately deported to killing centers. This distinction was extremely important. A document that did not provide freedom could still provide time, and time could create a chance for survival.

The rescue network faced constant danger. Nazi Germany controlled borders, transportation, communications, and much of the territory through which the documents had to travel. The people involved also had to deal with officials who might discover that the passports were not legitimate. The operation therefore depended on secrecy, trusted contacts, and the willingness of people in different countries to continue cooperating despite the risks.

The network expanded its efforts as the murder of European Jews became increasingly systematic. The Holocaust was not a single event but a process of persecution, forced displacement, ghettos, deportations, mass shootings, concentration camps, and killing centers. By the time the Ładoś Group was operating, millions of Jews were already in danger. The group could not stop the genocide, but it attempted to reach individuals and families who might still be saved.

The scale of the operation became clearer after the war. Historians and researchers studying surviving documents have identified thousands of people whose names appeared in the passport and certificate records connected with the group. The exact number of people actually saved is difficult to establish because some documents were never used, some recipients were still murdered, and records were destroyed during the war. It is therefore more accurate to speak of a rescue network that attempted to protect thousands rather than to claim that every person who received a document survived.

One important example was the use of Paraguayan documents by Jews in occupied Europe. Some recipients were eventually arrested and imprisoned because of their Jewish identity, but their foreign papers could affect where they were held and how they were treated. In some cases, people with such documents were grouped with other foreign nationals instead of being immediately deported to killing centers. The difference could give rescuers additional time to negotiate or find another opportunity for escape.

The Ładoś Group's work also shows how rescue during the Holocaust often depended on cooperation between people from very different backgrounds. Polish diplomats used their official positions and access to diplomatic facilities. Jewish activists collected names, gathered money, communicated with communities, and searched for people in danger. Other helpers carried messages and documents across borders. No single person controlled the entire operation.

The group continued its activities even as the situation around Switzerland became more difficult. Switzerland was neutral during the war, but it was surrounded by Axis-controlled or Axis-influenced territory. Information could be dangerous to transport, and the diplomats had to operate within the limits of their official positions. The rescue effort therefore required both diplomacy and secrecy.

Aleksander Ładoś was not the only person responsible for the rescue effort, and calling the entire operation simply "Ładoś's rescue mission" can hide the contributions of others. The name Ładoś Group is useful because it describes the network as a whole. Its work depended on Rokicki, Ryniewicz, Silberschein, Eiss, and many other people whose names are less well known.

After the war, the story of the group was not immediately widely known. Some of its records remained scattered, and the experiences of many recipients were lost with those who were killed. Later research brought greater attention to the documents preserved in archives and private collections. Researchers were able to connect names, passports, correspondence, and testimony to reconstruct parts of the operation.

The story also reminds us that paperwork can become a tool of both persecution and rescue. Nazi authorities used registration, identity documents, lists, and records to identify and control Jewish people. The Ładoś Group tried to use similar systems in the opposite direction, creating documents that could give threatened people another identity or legal connection.

The group did not have the power to defeat Nazi Germany or end the Holocaust. Its members worked within a limited and dangerous system, trying to save particular people while millions remained under threat. Some attempts failed. Some documents arrived too late. Some recipients were murdered despite possessing protection papers. Yet the effort demonstrated that diplomatic positions, community networks, and individual decisions could still be used to help people in extreme circumstances.

The Ładoś Group is remembered today because its members chose to act when Jewish lives were being systematically destroyed. Their story is not only about forged passports. It is about cooperation, secrecy, persistence, and the value of helping specific people even when the larger crisis seems impossible to stop.

The surviving records give historians a clearer picture of the people behind the operation and the thousands whose names passed through it. Each name represents an individual life rather than simply a number in a historical account.

The Ładoś Group's work shows that rescue could take many forms. It could happen through hiding, escape routes, food, money, shelter, or diplomatic protection. In Bern, it sometimes happened through a passport written by hand.

That is why the Ładoś Group belongs among the Hidden Legends.
