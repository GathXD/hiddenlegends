THE HIDDEN STORY

Katharine Blodgett

Born: January 10, 1898

Died: October 12, 1979

Field: Physics & Chemistry

Known For: Non-reflective glass

Before Katharine Burr Blodgett became known for making glass almost invisible, she was a young woman growing up at a time when scientific careers were still largely closed to women. Her achievements would eventually change optical technology, but her journey began long before her most famous discovery. It began with education, curiosity, determination, and a willingness to enter places where few women had gone before.

Katharine Blodgett was born on January 10, 1898, in Schenectady, New York. Her father, George Blodgett, was a patent attorney who worked with General Electric. He died shortly before Katharine was born, leaving her mother to raise the family. Despite the difficulties surrounding her early childhood, Katharine was given opportunities to develop her education and interests.

As she grew older, Katharine became particularly interested in mathematics and science. She attended Bryn Mawr College and studied physics, graduating in 1917. Her education did not end there. She continued her studies at the University of Chicago, where she earned a master's degree in physics in 1918.

Her master's research focused on charcoal and its ability to absorb gases. The research had practical importance because scientists were looking for better materials for gas masks during the First World War. This early experience showed Blodgett how scientific research could move beyond theory and become useful in solving real problems.

After completing her master's degree, Blodgett joined General Electric's research laboratory in Schenectady. She became the first woman scientist hired by the laboratory. At the time, this was an extraordinary achievement. Scientific laboratories were overwhelmingly male, and women often had to overcome assumptions that they were not suited for advanced technical work.

At General Electric, Blodgett worked with Irving Langmuir, a prominent scientist who would later win the Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Langmuir was investigating extremely thin layers of molecules that could form on the surface of water. Blodgett became deeply involved in this research and began developing her own expertise in thin molecular films.

Her work eventually led her to continue her education in England. She attended the University of Cambridge and studied at the Cavendish Laboratory. There she worked in an environment connected with some of the most important physics research of the era.

In 1926, Blodgett earned a PhD in physics from Cambridge. She became the first woman to receive a doctorate in physics from the university. The achievement was important not only because of the degree itself, but because it showed that a woman could reach the highest levels of scientific education and research.

After earning her doctorate, Blodgett returned to General Electric and continued her research with Langmuir. Her work focused on thin films, surfaces, and the interaction between materials and light. Although the layers she studied were incredibly thin, they could have surprisingly large effects on the behavior of a surface.

Blodgett discovered that these molecular layers could be transferred from the surface of water onto solid materials. By repeatedly transferring layers, scientists could create films with carefully controlled thicknesses. This process became known as the Langmuir-Blodgett technique.

The technique gave researchers a new way to construct and study materials at an extremely small scale. Instead of simply allowing a material to form naturally, scientists could control the number and arrangement of molecular layers. This opened new possibilities in surface science and materials research.

Blodgett eventually discovered an especially important application for these films. She realized that carefully controlled layers could be placed on glass to reduce the amount of light reflected from its surface.

Ordinary glass does not allow all light to pass straight through. Some light is reflected from the surface, creating glare and reducing the clarity of what can be seen through the glass. For optical instruments, this could be a serious problem.

Blodgett's solution was remarkably precise. She created molecular coatings with a thickness that caused reflected light waves to interfere with one another. The result was glass that reflected very little visible light and could appear almost invisible.

The invention became known as non-reflective or "invisible" glass. It was a major achievement because it took an idea from the microscopic world and turned it into a practical technology.

The potential applications were enormous. Non-reflective glass could make cameras, microscopes, projectors, telescopes, and other optical equipment more effective. Reducing glare meant that more light could pass through the glass, allowing people to see images more clearly.

Her work also became valuable during the Second World War. Non-reflective optical technology could be useful in military equipment, including cameras and submarine periscopes. Blodgett's research therefore demonstrated once again how scientific discoveries could become useful far beyond the laboratory where they were developed.

What made her research especially impressive was its precision. Blodgett learned how to control films at a scale far too small to be seen with the naked eye. Yet those microscopic layers could change the way an entire sheet of glass interacted with light.

Blodgett continued working at General Electric for most of her professional career. She became recognized for her work in thin films and surface chemistry and received several honors for her contributions to science.

In 1951, she received the Garvan Medal from the American Chemical Society. The award recognized her scientific achievements at a time when women were still significantly underrepresented in scientific research.

Her career was significant for another reason. Blodgett was not simply a scientist who happened to make an important discovery. She was a woman who entered scientific institutions at a time when women were frequently excluded from many professional opportunities.

She became General Electric's first woman scientist and Cambridge's first woman to receive a doctorate in physics. These achievements helped challenge the idea that advanced scientific research was primarily a man's profession.

Her work also demonstrated the importance of persistence. Blodgett did not make her most famous discovery immediately. It developed from years of studying surfaces, molecules, materials, and light. The invisible glass was the result of many experiments and a growing understanding of how extremely thin films could behave.

The Langmuir-Blodgett technique continued to be useful long after Blodgett's own experiments. Scientists could use the technique to create organized molecular films and investigate materials at very small scales. It became an important tool in research involving surfaces and thin-film technology.

Blodgett died in 1979, but the principles behind her research continued to influence science and technology. Her work demonstrated that something almost impossibly small could produce a visible effect on something as ordinary as a sheet of glass.

Today, anti-reflective coatings are common in many forms of optical technology. People may encounter them on eyeglasses, cameras, displays, lenses, and other equipment without ever thinking about the scientific discoveries that made them possible.

This is part of what makes Blodgett's story so interesting. Her work did not create a technology that constantly demanded attention. Instead, it helped remove something people noticed: unwanted reflection. Her invention made it easier to see through glass without thinking about the glass itself.

Her scientific legacy is therefore almost fittingly connected to invisibility. The more successful the technology became, the less people had to notice it. The coating worked quietly, allowing light to pass through more efficiently while remaining almost completely unnoticed.

Katharine Blodgett's life also reminds us that important scientific progress often comes from combining different areas of knowledge. Her work involved physics, chemistry, materials science, and optics. She studied molecular films and eventually found a way to use them to control light.

She did not become one of the most famous scientists of the twentieth century. Her name is much less widely recognized than many of the scientists who worked during the same era. Yet her discoveries became part of technologies used by millions of people.

Her story began with a young woman who wanted to understand science and continued through universities, laboratories, experiments, and years of research. Along the way, she broke barriers that had limited women in science and developed technology that changed how people use glass.

Katharine Blodgett showed that a scientist does not need to discover something enormous to change the world. Sometimes the most important discoveries happen at a scale too small for the human eye to see.

Her thin molecular films were almost invisible, but their impact was not. They helped improve optical technology, advanced the study of thin films, and provided future scientists with new ways to control materials at the molecular level.

More importantly, Blodgett's career showed that scientific ability should not be limited by gender. She entered a research laboratory where women were rare, earned a doctorate in physics at one of the world's leading universities, and went on to develop an invention with lasting practical value.

Her story deserves to be remembered because it combines discovery with determination. She did not simply study the world as it was. She found a way to change it, one molecular layer at a time.

Katharine Blodgett may have worked with materials that were almost impossible to see, but the legacy of her work can be found everywhere light passes through glass. Her discoveries helped make the invisible useful, and in doing so, she became one of the scientists whose contributions deserve to be seen.
