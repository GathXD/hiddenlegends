CHRISTIAN

Lorenzo Ruiz

The Filipino husband and father who fled Manila, journeyed to Japan, and became the first Filipino saint.

THE HIDDEN STORY

Lorenzo Ruiz

Born: November 28, 1594

Died: September 29, 1637

Field: Layman, Husband & Martyr

Known For: First Filipino Saint

Long before he became known as the first Filipino saint, Lorenzo Ruiz lived a life that seemed remarkably ordinary. He was born in Manila in 1594, in the district of Binondo, to a Chinese father and a Tagala mother. He grew up in a Catholic family and received his education from the Dominican friars. His early life was connected closely with the church, but there was nothing to suggest that he would eventually become one of the most important figures in Philippine Catholic history.

Lorenzo served as an altar boy and worked as a calligrapher and church clerk. He was known for copying documents and assisting with the activities of the parish. He also became associated with the Confraternity of the Most Holy Rosary, a Catholic organization connected with the Dominican community. His life was centered around work, family, and faith.

Lorenzo eventually married a Filipino woman named Rosario. Together they had three children. He was not a priest or missionary. He was a husband and father who worked to support his family and participated in the religious life of his community. The Vatican later emphasized this ordinary part of his life when describing him as a married layman with a family.

His life changed suddenly in 1636. Lorenzo became connected to a homicide case after being accused of involvement in the killing of a Spaniard. The historical record surrounding the accusation is limited. A Dominican account states that Lorenzo was being sought by the authorities because of a homicide at which he was present or which had been attributed to him. Fearing arrest, Lorenzo sought refuge with Dominican missionaries who were preparing to leave the Philippines.

The missionaries were preparing to travel to Japan. At the time, Japan was experiencing severe persecution of Christians under the Tokugawa shogunate. The missionaries knew that traveling there was dangerous, but Lorenzo decided to join them. His decision was not simply a religious pilgrimage. He was leaving behind his wife, his children, his home, and the familiar streets of Manila.

Lorenzo traveled with a group of Dominican missionaries that included Fathers Antonio Gonzalez, Guillermo Courtet, and Miguel de Aozaraza. The group also included other religious workers and companions. They eventually reached Okinawa, but their arrival placed them in the middle of an increasingly dangerous campaign against Christianity.

Christianity had already existed in Japan for generations, but by the seventeenth century the government had begun a systematic persecution of Christians. Missionaries were expelled, churches were destroyed, and Christians were pressured to abandon their faith. Those suspected of helping missionaries could be arrested, imprisoned, tortured, or executed.

Lorenzo and his companions were arrested after arriving in Japan. They were interrogated and imprisoned. The group was eventually taken to Nagasaki, one of the major centers of Christianity in Japan. There, the authorities attempted to force the prisoners to renounce their faith.

Lorenzo faced a situation completely different from the ordinary life he had known in Manila. He was no longer simply a church clerk, husband, or father. He was now a prisoner being questioned about his beliefs. The decision before him was severe: deny his Christian faith and possibly save his life, or continue professing it despite the danger.

According to the account presented by Pope John Paul II during Lorenzo's beatification, the judges questioned him about his faith. Lorenzo declared that he was a Christian and that he would die for God. The words attributed to him became one of the best-known parts of his story: even if he had thousands of lives, he would give them all rather than abandon Christ.

His response was significant because Lorenzo was not a trained theologian or a priest. He was a layman who had learned his faith through his family, parish, Dominican education, and Christian community in Manila. The Vatican described his final testimony as the completion of the Christian formation he had received in Binondo.

The prisoners were subjected to severe torture. One of the methods used by the authorities was known as the pit, or tsurushi. Victims were suspended upside down over a pit while their bodies were restrained. The method was designed to cause extreme suffering and pressure prisoners into renouncing Christianity.

Lorenzo endured the persecution without abandoning his faith. The exact details of every moment of his imprisonment are difficult to reconstruct because the surviving historical records are limited, but the broad outline of his martyrdom is well established. He and his companions were taken to the execution site at Nishizaka in Nagasaki.

Lorenzo died on September 29, 1637. He was approximately forty-two years old. His death took place thousands of kilometers from Manila, the city where he had been born, married, worked, and raised his family.

News of his death eventually reached Manila. The Dominican community recorded the martyrdom, and Lorenzo's name remained connected with the group of Christians who had died in Japan. Yet centuries would pass before he became formally recognized as a saint.

Lorenzo's story remained alive in the Catholic communities of the Philippines and among the Dominicans who had documented the martyrdom. His life was particularly significant because he represented something different from the European missionaries who had traveled to Asia. Lorenzo was a Filipino layman, born and raised in Manila, who had traveled to Japan with missionaries and died alongside them.

His heritage also reflected the diverse population of early Manila. His father was Chinese and his mother was Tagala. Binondo itself was an important center of Chinese-Filipino life and commerce. Lorenzo's background therefore connected several communities that were already part of the multicultural society of Manila.

In 1981, more than three hundred years after his death, Lorenzo was beatified by Pope John Paul II in Manila. The ceremony took place at Luneta Park on February 18. He became the first Filipino to be officially beatified by the Catholic Church.

During the beatification Mass, Pope John Paul II described Lorenzo as the first martyr of the young Church in Manila. He connected Lorenzo's life to the parish in Binondo and to the family in which his Christian faith had been lived and taught. The Pope also emphasized that Lorenzo's example was not only about dying for faith, but about living one's faith in ordinary life.

Six years later, Lorenzo's story reached another historic moment. On October 18, 1987, Pope John Paul II canonized Lorenzo Ruiz in St. Peter's Square in Rome. He became the first native-born Filipino saint officially recognized by the Catholic Church.

The canonization was a major event for the Philippines. The Philippine government declared the period leading to the canonization as Lorenzo Ruiz Month and proclaimed October 19, 1987, as Lorenzo Ruiz Day. The celebrations reflected the importance that his story had gained in the country.

Lorenzo's recognition did not erase the ordinary parts of his life. Instead, those parts became an important part of how he was remembered. He was a worker. He was a husband. He was a father. He was a church servant. He was also a man who suddenly found himself far from home and facing a decision that would determine the rest of his life.

This makes his story different from that of many famous missionaries. Lorenzo did not spend decades traveling from country to country. He did not establish a major institution or write a large collection of theological works. His historical importance came from his witness as an ordinary Filipino layman who remained faithful during persecution.

Today, Saint Lorenzo Ruiz is honored as the patron saint of the Philippines and is particularly associated with Filipino migrants, families, young people, altar servers, and people living far from home. His story has also become especially meaningful to Filipinos working overseas because Lorenzo himself left the Philippines and died in a foreign land.

Churches and schools throughout the Philippines and other countries bear his name. His image often shows him holding a rosary or cross and wearing clothing representing his Filipino heritage. These artistic representations help connect modern Filipino Catholics with a man who lived more than four centuries ago.

Yet the most important part of Lorenzo's story remains remarkably simple. He was an ordinary man whose life suddenly became extraordinary. He had a family waiting for him in Manila, but circumstances led him onto a ship bound for Japan. There, in the middle of persecution, he was forced to decide what he was willing to give up for his beliefs.

Lorenzo's answer became the part of his life that survived the centuries. He refused to abandon his faith even when doing so might have saved his life. His death in Nagasaki became the event through which he would eventually be remembered throughout the Philippines and around the world.

More than three hundred years after Lorenzo died, he became the first Filipino saint. His journey from Binondo to Nagasaki had ended centuries earlier, but his story had continued traveling—from Dominican records, to the Catholic communities of Manila, to his beatification in 1981, and finally to his canonization in 1987.

Lorenzo Ruiz reminds us that history does not always remember people because they were powerful. Sometimes it remembers someone because, when faced with a choice, that person remained faithful to what he believed. His life began as the story of an ordinary husband and father in Binondo. It ended with his name becoming part of the history of the Philippines, Japan, and the Catholic Church.

“So everyone who acknowledges me before men, I also will acknowledge before my Father who is in heaven.” Matthew 10:32-33

This fits Lorenzo particularly well because he acknowledged his faith in Christ even when it meant leaving his family and facing death.
