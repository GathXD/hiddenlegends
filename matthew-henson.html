Matthew Henson

The Arctic explorer who traveled farther north than most people of his generation and played a crucial role in the expedition that reached the North Pole.

THE HIDDEN STORY

Matthew Henson

Born: August 8, 1866

Died: March 9, 1955

Field: Exploration, Arctic Travel & Navigation

Known For: Arctic expeditions with Robert Peary

Matthew Henson was born into a world that offered him very few reasons to believe he would one day become an internationally recognized explorer. Yet through determination, practical skill, and years of difficult travel, he became one of the most important members of the Arctic expeditions led by Robert Peary. His work helped make possible one of the most famous exploration attempts of the early twentieth century.

Henson was born on August 8, 1866, in Nanjemoy, Maryland. His parents, Lemuel and Caroline Henson, were African American sharecroppers. Henson's mother died when he was young, and his father later moved the family to Washington, D.C. After becoming orphaned as a child, Henson had to become independent at an early age.

As a teenager, Henson traveled to Baltimore and found work as a cabin boy aboard a sailing ship. The ship's captain, Captain Childs, became an important teacher to him. Henson learned navigation, seamanship, and the practical skills required to survive at sea. His experience aboard ships introduced him to a world far larger than the one he had known as a child.

After Captain Childs died, Henson eventually returned to Washington, D.C., where he worked in a clothing store. It was there that he met Robert E. Peary, a U.S. Navy officer and engineer who was planning expeditions to Central America and eventually the Arctic. Peary recognized Henson's abilities and hired him as a valet and assistant.

The relationship would last for more than two decades. Henson became much more than a servant or assistant. He was an experienced sailor, craftsman, navigator, and traveler. He learned how to repair equipment, build and manage sledges, handle dogs, and communicate with Inuit communities. These skills would become essential as Peary's expeditions moved farther into the Arctic.

Henson accompanied Peary on expeditions to Greenland beginning in the 1890s. The journeys were extremely difficult. Arctic explorers faced freezing temperatures, dangerous sea ice, long periods of darkness, limited food, and the constant possibility of equipment failure. Henson adapted to the environment and gradually became one of the expedition's most dependable members.

Henson's knowledge of Arctic travel grew through years of experience. He learned techniques from Inuit hunters and travelers, including how to construct and use sledges, manage teams of dogs, build shelters, and travel efficiently across snow and sea ice. These practical skills were often more important to survival than theoretical knowledge.

During the expeditions, Henson also became familiar with the Inuit language and developed relationships with members of Inuit communities. Inuit knowledge was essential to many Arctic expeditions because local people understood the environment, weather, animals, and sea ice in ways that outsiders could not.

Peary's expeditions repeatedly attempted to travel farther north. The team faced enormous obstacles. Routes could disappear when sea ice shifted, open water could block progress, and supplies had to be carefully calculated. A mistake could leave explorers hundreds of miles from safety with little food or equipment.

Henson's role became increasingly important. He could travel efficiently by sledge, repair equipment, and help lead dog teams. His ability to work in extreme conditions made him an essential member of the expedition. Although racial discrimination limited the recognition he received, his companions understood that his practical abilities were extremely valuable.

In 1908, Peary launched another major attempt to reach the geographic North Pole. The expedition traveled north from Greenland toward the pole. Henson was once again part of the team. The journey became a test of endurance unlike almost anything Henson had experienced before.

The expedition used a system of advance parties to transport supplies and prepare the route. As the team progressed north, some members turned back while others continued. Eventually, a smaller group approached the final stretch toward the pole.

Henson was among the small group that continued with Peary. According to Henson's own account, he reached what he believed was the North Pole ahead of Peary after becoming separated from the others. Peary and Henson then completed the final approach together, accompanied by Inuit members of the expedition.

On April 6, 1909, Peary reported that the expedition had reached the North Pole. The achievement immediately became controversial. Frederick Cook, another American explorer, claimed that he had reached the pole earlier, and later historians have debated the evidence surrounding both claims. Modern historical assessments generally regard Peary's claim as more credible, although some details of the exact location reached remain debated.

Regardless of the continuing debate over the exact position reached by the expedition, Henson's role was significant. He had spent years traveling through one of the harshest environments on Earth and had become an essential member of the team. His Arctic experience, technical skills, and ability to work with Inuit guides contributed greatly to the expedition's survival.

Returning to the United States did not immediately bring Henson the recognition he deserved. Peary received much greater public attention, while Henson's contribution was often treated as secondary. Racial prejudice played a major role in limiting the recognition available to an African American explorer in the early twentieth century.

Henson eventually published his own account of his experiences in a book titled A Negro Explorer at the North Pole in 1912. The book described his life and his years of Arctic exploration. It provided readers with his perspective on the expeditions and helped preserve his place in the history of polar exploration.

Recognition came slowly. Henson received several honors later in life, although much of the attention surrounding his achievements came decades after his greatest expedition. In 1937, he became the first African American to receive membership in the Explorers Club, an organization that had previously been difficult for people like Henson to enter.

In 1944, Henson received a Peary Polar Expedition Medal from the U.S. government. He also received other honors during his final years. His contribution gradually became more widely recognized as historians and the public began examining the North Pole expedition more carefully.

Matthew Henson died on March 9, 1955, in New York City at the age of eighty-eight. He was initially buried in Woodlawn Cemetery in the Bronx. Years later, his remains and those of his wife were moved to Arlington National Cemetery, where he was honored near Robert Peary.

What makes Matthew Henson a Hidden Legend is that his achievement was for many years overshadowed by the person who received most of the public credit. Henson was not simply someone who accompanied Peary. He was an experienced Arctic traveler whose practical knowledge became essential to the expedition's ability to survive and move across the frozen north.

His story is also a powerful example of perseverance in the face of discrimination. Henson was an African American man traveling through an era when racism severely restricted opportunities in American society. Even after participating in one of the most famous exploration attempts of his time, he did not immediately receive recognition equal to his contribution.

Henson's success was built on skills he had developed throughout his life. His years at sea taught him navigation and discipline. His work with Peary taught him Arctic travel. His relationships with Inuit communities helped him understand how to survive in a difficult environment. Each experience prepared him for the challenges that awaited him farther north.

His story also demonstrates the importance of knowledge that is sometimes overlooked. Arctic exploration depended heavily on Indigenous knowledge and technologies. Inuit expertise in transportation, clothing, shelter, hunting, and survival was essential to successful travel across the region. Henson learned from and worked closely with Inuit people, making those skills part of his own ability to survive.

The story of the North Pole expedition is complicated, and the exact historical details continue to be examined. Yet there is little doubt that Henson endured years of difficult Arctic expeditions and played a major practical role in Peary's exploration efforts. His experience was not created by one dramatic moment at the end of a journey. It was built through years of preparation, hardship, and repeated attempts.

Henson's life reminds us that recognition and achievement are not always the same thing. A person can make an enormous contribution without immediately receiving the credit they deserve. Sometimes history takes decades to recognize the people who worked behind the scenes.

Matthew Henson eventually became a symbol of determination, exploration, and perseverance. His journey from an orphaned boy in Maryland to an Arctic explorer demonstrates how far a person can travel when opportunity is combined with preparation and persistence.

His legacy is especially meaningful because he opened a path that many people of his background had rarely been allowed to enter. He demonstrated that exploration was not limited by race or social position, even though the society around him often tried to impose those limitations.

Matthew Henson spent decades traveling toward places few people had ever seen. The cold, isolation, and danger of the Arctic tested him repeatedly, but he continued. His name may once have stood in the shadow of a more famous explorer, but his own story deserves to be remembered.

The North Pole was only one destination. The greater story was the journey that made reaching it possible: years of learning, difficult expeditions, cooperation with Inuit communities, technical skill, physical endurance, and persistence. Matthew Henson was part of all of it.

His life reminds us that history is often written around the names that became famous, while the people who made great achievements possible can disappear into the background. Matthew Henson's story brings one of those people back into the light. He was an explorer, sailor, craftsman, and survivor whose determination carried him farther north than most people of his generation could imagine.
