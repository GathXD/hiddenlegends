THE HIDDEN STORY

Frank Foley

Born: 24 November, 1884

Died: 8 May, 1958

Field: Intelligence & Diplomacy

Known For: Secret Rescue of Jewish Refugees

In the darkest years of twentieth-century Europe, when governments were closing their borders and millions of people were searching for somewhere to escape, one quiet British official used his position to help people who had almost nowhere else to turn. His name was Frank Foley. He was not a soldier leading an army, a politician giving speeches, or a famous resistance commander. He was a British passport officer working in Berlin. Yet behind the ordinary appearance of his job, Foley was secretly helping Jewish people escape Nazi Germany.

Born in Liverpool, England, in 1884, Foley originally followed a very different path. He studied classics and philosophy and later trained for the priesthood before deciding not to become a Catholic priest. He eventually entered British intelligence and began working for the Secret Intelligence Service, better known as MI6. His intelligence career took him to different parts of Europe, where he worked quietly gathering information and protecting British interests.

In 1922, Foley was sent to Berlin. Officially, he worked as a passport control officer at the British Embassy. His position gave him an unusual opportunity. Because Britain controlled the issuing of visas and travel documents, desperate people fleeing Nazi persecution often came to officials like Foley hoping for permission to leave Germany. As Adolf Hitler and the Nazi Party strengthened their control, the situation became increasingly dangerous for Germany's Jewish population.

After Hitler became Chancellor of Germany in 1933, Nazi persecution rapidly intensified. Jewish businesses were boycotted, Jewish people were stripped of rights, and discriminatory laws made ordinary life increasingly difficult. Later, violence became even more severe. Foley witnessed the growing danger around him and understood that official procedures alone would not be enough to protect people who were being hunted.

Foley began bending the rules to help people obtain the documents they needed to escape. He sometimes issued visas to people who might not normally have qualified. He searched for legal ways to speed up applications and helped applicants understand what documents they needed. When necessary, he went beyond what was expected of someone working behind a government desk.

His actions became especially important after the November 1938 pogrom known as Kristallnacht. Across Germany and Austria, Jewish homes, businesses, and synagogues were attacked. Thousands of Jewish men were arrested and sent to concentration camps. The violence made it clear that the danger was no longer simply discrimination or political persecution. For many Jewish families, leaving Germany had become a matter of survival.

Foley worked with Jewish organizations and individuals who were trying to escape. He helped arrange travel documents and sometimes provided assistance to people who were already in hiding. According to later accounts, he even helped some people obtain passports through unofficial methods and found ways to get them out of Germany before Nazi authorities could stop them.

His work was dangerous because Foley himself was under surveillance. The Nazi government had its own security organizations, including the Gestapo, and foreign officials working in Berlin were not free to operate without scrutiny. Foley was also secretly connected to British intelligence. His official position gave him diplomatic protection, but it did not make him invulnerable.

What makes Foley's story remarkable is that he did not act like a traditional hero. He did not openly challenge the Nazi government with speeches or weapons. Instead, he used paperwork, official authority, personal connections, and careful decisions. A passport stamp might seem insignificant, but for someone trapped inside Nazi Germany, a valid document could mean the difference between freedom and imprisonment.

Foley's efforts were not limited to people who could easily afford to leave. He tried to help those who had few resources and little influence. He sometimes took enormous personal risks by entering places where Jewish prisoners were being held or by intervening on behalf of people who were in immediate danger. His position allowed him to negotiate with German authorities and create opportunities for people to escape.

The exact number of people Foley helped is difficult to determine. Historians and organizations have credited him with saving hundreds of Jewish lives, while some estimates have placed the number even higher. Because Foley kept much of his work secret, there is no complete list of everyone he assisted. He did not keep detailed records for the purpose of receiving recognition, and many of the people he helped disappeared into new lives outside Germany.

In 1939, as Europe moved toward war, Foley was forced to leave Berlin. Britain and Germany were approaching open conflict, and diplomatic relations were collapsing. Foley returned to Britain and continued working for British intelligence during the Second World War. His secret career remained largely unknown to the public.

After the war, Foley lived quietly. The full extent of what he had done in Berlin was not immediately known, partly because intelligence work was kept secret. He died in 1958, having spent most of his life without seeking public recognition for the people he had helped.

Years later, survivors and researchers began telling the story of the British official who had risked his career and personal safety to protect people targeted by the Nazi regime. Their accounts helped reveal a very different picture of Foley from the quiet government official he had appeared to be.

In 1999, Frank Foley was recognized by Yad Vashem as a "Righteous Among the Nations," an honor given to non-Jews who risked their lives to save Jews during the Holocaust. The recognition placed his name among people who had chosen to help others despite living under extraordinarily dangerous circumstances.

Foley's story is important because his power did not come from having an army behind him. His greatest weapon was the authority he already possessed and the courage to use it for people who had been denied protection. He understood that rules could be used to exclude people, but they could also sometimes be interpreted in ways that opened a path to freedom.

His story also shows how difficult it can be to recognize courage while history is happening. Foley was not famous during the years when he was helping people. Most of the people around him probably saw an ordinary British official doing ordinary diplomatic work. Behind that appearance, however, he was making decisions that could determine whether another person lived, escaped, or was captured.

Frank Foley never commanded thousands of people, never stood before a cheering crowd, and never became a household name. Instead, he worked quietly in a city where silence could be dangerous and where one wrong decision could have devastating consequences. His courage came from choosing to act when doing nothing would have been easier and safer.

Today, Foley is remembered as one of the many lesser-known people who resisted the machinery of persecution through individual acts of courage. His life reminds us that changing history does not always require a position of great power. Sometimes it begins with one person deciding that another person's life is worth protecting. Frank Foley had the opportunity to look away. Instead, he chose to help—and for hundreds of people, that choice meant the possibility of another tomorrow.
