THE HIDDEN STORY

Eilene Galloway

Born: May 4, 1906

Died: May 2, 2009

Field: Space Law & Policy

Known For: Helping shape NASA and international space law

When people think about the beginning of the Space Age, they often remember rockets, astronauts, engineers, and scientists. But launching humanity into space required more than technology. Governments needed to decide how space would be used, who would be responsible for it, and what rules should guide nations as they moved beyond Earth. One of the people who helped answer those questions was Eilene Marie Galloway.

Eilene Galloway was born on May 4, 1906, in Kansas City, Missouri. She grew up during a period when aviation was still developing and spaceflight existed only in imagination. The idea that people might someday travel beyond Earth's atmosphere seemed more like science fiction than a political problem.

Her father, Joseph Locke Slack, worked for the railroad and later served in the United States Marine Corps. After he left the household, Eilene was raised primarily by her mother, Lottie Rose Harris. From an early age, she developed an interest in public affairs, debate, and government.

Galloway attended Westport High School in Kansas City, where she became captain of the debate team. Debate taught her something that would become extremely valuable throughout her career: difficult problems could be examined from different points of view, and complicated arguments could be broken down into understandable ideas.

She later attended Washington University in St. Louis on a scholarship. Her studies were interrupted when she married George Barnes Galloway in 1924. She moved to Washington, D.C., where her husband was studying government and economics.

Moving to Washington placed Galloway close to the center of American government. Although she did not begin her career as a space specialist, she developed expertise in government policy, national security, legislation, and international relations.

In 1941, Galloway began working for the Legislative Reference Service of the Library of Congress, an organization that provided research and information to members of Congress. The service would later become the Congressional Research Service.

Her work required her to research complicated national issues and prepare reports that lawmakers could use when making decisions. She eventually became a National Defense Analyst and later a Senior Specialist in International Relations dealing with national security.

Galloway worked on subjects including military organization, atomic energy, and guided missiles. These topics seemed far removed from space law, but the rapid development of missiles would soon connect national defense with a completely new question: what would happen when technology allowed nations to operate beyond Earth's atmosphere?

The turning point came in October 1957, when the Soviet Union launched Sputnik 1, the first artificial satellite to orbit Earth. The small satellite's radio signal could be heard around the world, but its importance was much larger than its size.

Sputnik demonstrated that the Soviet Union possessed powerful rocket technology capable of placing objects into orbit. American leaders immediately began asking whether the same technology could also be used for military purposes. The United States was suddenly facing a problem that involved science, national security, international politics, and the future of exploration.

Senator Lyndon B. Johnson, who was leading congressional investigations into American preparedness, asked Galloway to help analyze the situation. Her previous work on missiles and national defense made her particularly useful as Congress tried to understand what Sputnik meant.

Galloway helped prepare research and analyze testimony during congressional hearings. At first, the discussion centered heavily on military competition. But testimony from scientists and engineers began to show that space could offer much more than military advantages.

Satellites could improve weather forecasting. Space technology could support communications. Scientific instruments could study Earth and other planets. Human beings might eventually travel to the Moon and perhaps beyond. The question was no longer simply how America should respond to the Soviet Union. It had become a question about what humanity should do with the new environment beyond Earth.

Galloway became involved with Representative John W. McCormack and the House Select Committee on Astronautics and Space Exploration. The committee examined how the United States should organize its space activities.

One important question was whether America needed a new organization devoted to space. Galloway contributed her policy knowledge and analysis to the discussions surrounding the legislation.

When the proposed legislation was being developed, Galloway made an important suggestion about the name and structure of the future organization. She recommended using the word "Administration" rather than simply "Agency." The distinction reflected her belief that the organization needed broad authority to coordinate space activities across the federal government.

The recommendation was accepted. In 1958, President Dwight D. Eisenhower signed the National Aeronautics and Space Act, creating the National Aeronautics and Space Administration, better known as NASA.

Galloway did not build a rocket, calculate an orbital trajectory, or fly into space. Her contribution was different. She helped create the legal and organizational framework that allowed the United States to coordinate its growing space program.

Her role demonstrated something important about major technological achievements: progress does not come only from the people operating machines. Behind every major scientific program are people who write laws, establish institutions, analyze evidence, and decide how new technology should be governed.

After NASA was created, Galloway's work expanded from American space policy to international space law. She understood that space could not simply become another battlefield where nations competed without limits.

If satellites and spacecraft were going to cross national boundaries and operate beyond Earth's atmosphere, countries would need agreements governing their activities. Questions about responsibility, peaceful exploration, rescue of astronauts, liability for damage, and the use of space resources would all eventually have to be addressed.

Galloway became deeply involved with the United Nations Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space, commonly known as COPUOS. She participated in the development and discussion of principles that would become part of the international legal framework for space.

During the 1960s, she worked on international agreements dealing with space activities. One of the most important was the Outer Space Treaty, which was opened for signature in 1967. The treaty established major principles for the exploration and use of outer space, including the idea that space should be explored and used for peaceful purposes and for the benefit of all countries.

Galloway later described the treaty as a foundation for space law. She worked through complicated negotiations and helped analyze different proposals so that lawmakers could understand their meaning and consequences.

Her work also involved agreements concerning the rescue and return of astronauts, liability for damage caused by space objects, and registration of objects launched into space. These subjects may sound technical, but they addressed real problems that emerged as humanity began placing increasingly large numbers of objects into orbit.

Galloway was also a founding member of the International Institute of Space Law. The organization provided a place where lawyers, scholars, scientists, policymakers, and other experts could study the legal questions created by space exploration.

She continued working with space policy for decades. Although she formally retired from the Library of Congress in 1975, she did not simply leave the field behind. She continued serving as a consultant and remained active in international space-law discussions well into old age.

Her influence was eventually recognized by organizations across the aerospace and legal communities. In 1984, she received the NASA Public Service Award and Gold Medal. In 1987, she became the first recipient of a Lifetime Achievement Award from Women in Aerospace.

She later became a fellow of the American Astronautical Society and, in 2006, became the first woman elected an Honorary Fellow of the American Institute of Aeronautics and Astronautics.

Even as she received these honors, Galloway remained careful not to describe the development of space law as the achievement of one person. She emphasized that many people and nations contributed to the creation of the international rules governing space.

That humility is part of what makes her story especially interesting. Galloway worked in a field where success depended on negotiation rather than dramatic individual victories. Her work often involved reading documents, analyzing testimony, comparing legal proposals, and helping lawmakers understand difficult questions.

Yet those quiet tasks could have enormous consequences. The institutions and agreements developed during the early Space Age helped establish a framework for countries to cooperate in an environment that belonged to no single nation.

Eilene Galloway died on May 2, 2009, in Washington, D.C., just two days before her 103rd birthday. By then, humanity had already walked on the Moon, built permanent space stations, sent robotic spacecraft to the outer planets, and filled Earth's orbit with satellites.

The world of 2009 was almost unimaginable compared with the world she entered in 1906. When Galloway was born, powered flight itself was still in its infancy. By the end of her life, humans had been living and working in space for years.

Her career connected those two worlds. She helped governments understand the implications of technologies that had not existed when she was young, and she helped create systems for managing those technologies responsibly.

What makes Galloway a Hidden Legend is that her contribution to space exploration cannot be captured in a photograph of a rocket launch. Her work happened in offices, hearings, committees, and international meetings. She helped shape the decisions that made space exploration possible on a national and international scale.

Her story reminds us that exploration requires more than courage and technology. It also requires rules, cooperation, diplomacy, and people willing to think about consequences before they become crises.

The rockets of the Space Age carried astronauts upward, but people like Eilene Galloway helped build the framework underneath them. She understood that humanity's future in space would eventually involve many nations, and that the choices made at the beginning of the Space Age could influence generations to come.

Long before private spacecraft, satellite constellations, and modern commercial spaceflight became realities, Galloway was already asking questions about how space should be governed. Her work helped establish the idea that the exploration of space should be accompanied by international cooperation and law.

Eilene Galloway never needed to leave Earth to become a pioneer of the Space Age. Her contribution was made through ideas, analysis, legislation, and diplomacy. She helped shape the institutions and principles that would guide people who did travel beyond Earth.

Her legacy lives in a simple but powerful idea: when humanity enters a new frontier, it must decide not only what it can do, but also how it should do it.

“The plans of the diligent lead surely to advantage.” Proverbs 21:5

Because she used careful research and wisdom to help shape the laws and policies that guided humanity’s future in space, making Proverbs 21:5 a fitting reflection of her work and character.
