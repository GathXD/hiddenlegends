THE HIDDEN STORY

William Carey

Born: August 17, 1761

Died: June 9, 1834

Field: Missions & Linguistics

Known For: Bible Translation in India

In the late eighteenth century, a poor English shoemaker became convinced that Christians should take the message of the Gospel beyond the borders of Europe. His name was William Carey. He had no wealthy family, powerful position, or university education to open doors for him. Instead, he taught himself languages, worked with his hands, studied the Bible, and gradually developed a vision that would help transform Protestant missionary work around the world.

William Carey was born on August 17, 1761, in the village of Paulerspury, Northamptonshire, England. His father was a weaver and parish clerk, and the family lived a modest life. William became an apprentice shoemaker as a teenager. While working in the shoemaker's shop, he developed a passion for reading and learning. He studied Latin, Greek, Hebrew, and other subjects largely on his own, using whatever books and resources he could find.

Carey also experienced a deep spiritual change during his younger years. He became a Baptist and began preaching in small churches and villages. His education was unusual because it did not come from an expensive institution. Much of his knowledge was acquired through determination and self-study. He believed that if something was important, he should be willing to work hard to understand it.

During this period, Carey became increasingly concerned about people around the world who had never heard the Christian message. Many Christians in Britain believed that missionary work was either unnecessary or impossible. Carey disagreed. In 1792, he preached a famous sermon calling Christians to "Expect great things from God; attempt great things for God." The sermon became associated with the growing missionary movement in Britain.

Later that year, the Baptist Missionary Society was formed. Carey volunteered to become a missionary and prepared to travel to India. The decision meant leaving behind the familiar life he had known. In 1793, Carey, his wife Dorothy, their children, and another missionary traveled to India. Their journey marked the beginning of decades of difficult and influential work.

Carey arrived in India at a time when British authorities were suspicious of missionaries. He initially struggled to establish himself and spent years working in different places to support his family. Eventually, he joined a missionary community at Serampore, near Calcutta, with Joshua Marshman and William Ward. Together they became known as the Serampore missionaries.

Carey believed that sharing the Christian message required understanding the languages of the people he hoped to reach. He devoted enormous amounts of time to translation. He learned Bengali and became involved in translating the Bible and other Christian materials into Indian languages. His work eventually extended into several languages, making printed religious texts available to people who had previously had little access to them.

Translation was only one part of Carey's work. He was deeply interested in education and believed that ordinary people should have opportunities to learn. The Serampore missionaries established schools and eventually helped create Serampore College. Carey also worked with Indian scholars and printers, recognizing that local knowledge was essential to producing accurate translations and educational materials.

Carey developed interests far beyond theology. He studied plants, agriculture, languages, and Indian society. He helped establish a botanical garden and worked with agricultural ideas that he believed could improve people's lives. He also became involved in printing and publishing newspapers and other materials. His work reflected a broad belief that education, literacy, science, and Christian faith could work together.

One issue that deeply concerned Carey was the suffering of vulnerable people. He strongly opposed the practice of sati, in which a widow could be expected to die on her husband's funeral pyre. Carey and other reformers campaigned against the practice. In 1829, the British government in India formally prohibited sati in territories under its authority. Carey's role in the campaign became part of his wider reputation as someone who wanted social change as well as religious conversion.

Carey's work was not easy. His family experienced serious hardship, including illness and personal tragedy. His young son Peter died, and Dorothy suffered severe mental distress. Carey also faced the destruction of much of his life's work when a fire broke out at the Serampore printing operation in 1812. Printing equipment, manuscripts, dictionaries, and years of translation work were destroyed.

The loss was devastating, but Carey and his colleagues refused to abandon their work. They rebuilt the printing operation and began recovering and reproducing materials that had been destroyed. Instead of allowing the disaster to end their mission, they treated it as another challenge that had to be overcome.

Over the years, Carey's influence continued to grow. His translation work helped establish a tradition of Protestant Bible translation into Indian languages, while his educational efforts influenced later generations of missionaries and educators. He also encouraged the study of Indian languages and cultures at a time when many Europeans knew very little about them.

Carey spent more than forty years in India. He faced financial uncertainty, family tragedy, language barriers, political restrictions, and the enormous challenge of building institutions almost from the beginning. Yet he continued working. His life demonstrated that missionary work, in his understanding, could involve education, translation, printing, scientific study, and practical service.

Carey died on June 9, 1834, at Serampore. He was seventy-two years old. He was buried there, near the community where he had spent much of his life. By the time of his death, the missionary movement he had helped inspire had expanded far beyond what he could have imagined when he was a poor shoemaker in England.

William Carey's legacy is especially important in the history of Protestant missions. He is often called the "father of modern missions" because of the influence his life had on organized missionary movements. His example encouraged other Christians to consider missionary service in different parts of the world. Missionary history is also complicated, however, because Christian missions sometimes operated alongside European colonial systems. Carey's work should therefore be remembered within that larger historical context.

What makes William Carey a Hidden Legend is the distance between where he started and what he eventually accomplished. He began as a village shoemaker without the advantages normally associated with great scholars or leaders. Through self-education, determination, and faith, he became a translator, educator, printer, linguist, and missionary whose work influenced generations after him.

His story also demonstrates the importance of perseverance. When people questioned whether missionary work was possible, Carey continued. When he struggled to establish himself in India, he continued. When disease and personal tragedy struck his family, he continued. When a devastating fire destroyed years of work, he helped rebuild rather than giving up.

Carey believed that faith should produce action. He did not limit his work to words. He learned languages, established schools, printed books, studied science, supported social reform, and worked with people from different cultural backgrounds. His approach helped create a model of missionary work that combined religious conviction with education and practical service.

His life reminds us that great influence does not always begin with great resources. William Carey started with very little, but he possessed a willingness to learn and a determination to use what he had. His journey from an English shoemaker's workshop to the mission fields of India became an example for countless people who believed that one ordinary person could make a lasting difference.

William Carey did not know how far his efforts would reach when he first decided to become a missionary. He simply believed that the work was worth doing and that he should begin. His life became a story of faith, learning, sacrifice, and perseverance. Long after his death, the schools, translations, publications, and missionary movements connected to his work continued to influence people around the world.

“Go ye therefore, and teach all nations.” Matthew 28:19

This fits Carey especially well because he is remembered for his missionary work in India, Bible translation, and his commitment to spreading Christianity.
