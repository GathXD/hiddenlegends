Alice
Ball

The chemist who developed an injectable treatment for Hansen's disease from chaulmoogra oil.

THE HIDDEN STORY

Born: 1892

Died: 1916

Field: Chemistry & Pharmaceutical Chemistry

Known For: The Ball Method and treatment for Hansen's disease

In the early twentieth century, Hansen's disease, also known as leprosy, was a frightening illness with few effective treatments. People diagnosed with the disease were often separated from their families and communities, and those living with advanced cases could suffer severe nerve damage and disability. At a time when medical science had limited answers, a young chemist named Alice Augusta Ball began working on a treatment that would change the lives of thousands of people.

Alice Ball was born on July 24, 1892, in Seattle, Washington. She grew up in a family that valued education and intellectual work. Her father, James Presley Ball Jr., was a newspaper editor, photographer, and lawyer, while her mother, Laura Louise Howard, came from a family involved in photography and business. Her grandfather, James Presley Ball Sr., was a well-known African American photographer.

Growing up in Seattle exposed Ball to a world of education, science, and photography. She attended Seattle High School and graduated in 1910. She then entered the University of Washington, where she studied pharmaceutical chemistry and pharmacy.

Ball excelled in her studies. She earned a bachelor's degree in pharmaceutical chemistry in 1912 and another bachelor's degree in pharmacy in 1914. Her academic achievements were remarkable considering the racial and gender barriers facing Black women in higher education and professional science at the time.

After completing her undergraduate education, Ball received a scholarship to continue her studies at the College of Hawaii, which later became the University of Hawaii. She moved to the islands and began graduate research in chemistry.

In 1915, Ball became the first woman and the first African American to earn a master's degree from the College of Hawaii. Her research focused on the chemistry of the kava plant, a plant traditionally used in the Pacific. Her ability to isolate and study active chemical compounds demonstrated her talent for pharmaceutical research.

Her work attracted the attention of Dr. Harry T. Hollmann, a physician working at the Kalihi Hospital in Honolulu. Hollmann was searching for a better way to treat Hansen's disease. At the time, chaulmoogra oil was one of the most promising treatments available, but it had a serious problem: the oil did not mix easily with water, which made it difficult to administer effectively.

Hollmann believed that Ball's knowledge of chemistry could help solve the problem. She began investigating the chemical structure of the oil and searching for a method of transforming its active compounds into a form that the human body could use more effectively.

Ball's challenge was not simply to find a substance that might work. She needed to understand the chemistry of chaulmoogra oil well enough to separate its active fatty acids and transform them into compounds that could be more easily absorbed and injected. This required careful laboratory experimentation and a deep understanding of organic chemistry.

Ball developed a method for modifying the fatty acids in chaulmoogra oil so that they could be converted into water-soluble derivatives. The resulting preparation could be injected into patients rather than relying on the difficult and unpleasant use of the raw oil.

The technique became known as the Ball Method. It represented an important improvement in the treatment of Hansen's disease. For patients who had previously faced isolation and very limited medical options, the treatment offered genuine hope.

Ball's research was especially impressive because she was only in her early twenties. She had become a university instructor and researcher while also carrying out difficult pharmaceutical investigations. She was breaking barriers not only as a Black scientist but also as a woman working in chemistry during an era when scientific institutions were overwhelmingly male.

Unfortunately, Ball did not live long enough to see the full impact of her discovery. In 1916, she became seriously ill. The exact nature of her illness is uncertain, although historical accounts have suggested that she may have been exposed to chlorine while demonstrating laboratory techniques.

Alice Ball died on December 31, 1916, at only twenty-four years old. Her death ended a promising scientific career before she had the opportunity to continue developing her research.

After her death, the importance of her work became complicated by the actions of another scientist. Arthur L. Dean, the president of the College of Hawaii and a chemist, continued research involving chaulmoogra oil. He published work describing the improved treatment but did not properly credit Ball for developing the underlying method.

For many years, Dean's name became associated with the treatment, while Ball's contribution was largely forgotten. Hollmann, however, recognized her role and published information explaining that the method had originally been developed by Ball.

This part of Ball's story reveals another challenge faced by women and minority scientists: making a discovery was not always enough to guarantee that history would remember who made it. Scientific recognition depended on institutions, publications, and the willingness of others to acknowledge the work of researchers who were often overlooked.

Despite the injustice surrounding the credit for her research, the Ball Method continued to be used for many years. It became one of the most effective treatments available for Hansen's disease before the development and widespread use of sulfone drugs and, later, multidrug antibiotic therapy.

Patients at hospitals in Hawaii and elsewhere received chaulmoogra-based preparations using methods based on Ball's research. Her work helped transform a difficult natural oil into a practical medical treatment.

For decades after her death, Alice Ball remained largely unknown outside specialist historical and medical circles. Her scientific achievement was overshadowed by the lack of recognition she had received during her short lifetime.

Researchers and historians eventually began bringing her story back into public attention. As historians examined the records of early medical research in Hawaii, they found evidence showing that Ball had developed the chemical process that made the treatment practical.

In 2000, the University of Hawaii formally honored Ball by placing a commemorative plaque on the campus. Hawaii Governor Mazie Hirono also proclaimed February 29, 2000, as Alice Ball Day. Because February 29 occurs only during leap years, the date became an unusual but memorable way to honor a scientist whose life had been far too short.

Ball's legacy has continued to grow since then. The University of Hawaii has recognized her as an important figure in the institution's scientific history, and her story has increasingly appeared in discussions about women and African Americans in science.

Her achievement is particularly important because of the people her work helped. Hansen's disease had long carried enormous social stigma. Patients were frequently treated as if they were dangerous simply because doctors did not understand the disease well and effective treatment was limited.

By improving the delivery of chaulmoogra oil, Ball helped make treatment more practical and gave patients greater hope of recovery. Her chemistry did not eliminate Hansen's disease, and the Ball Method was eventually replaced by newer medicines, but it played an important role during a period when treatment options were extremely limited.

Ball's story is also a reminder of how quickly a person's historical legacy can disappear. She died before reaching the age of twenty-five, and her most important work was later associated with another scientist. For much of the twentieth century, the young chemist who had created the method remained in the shadows.

Yet the evidence of her work survived. Her laboratory research, academic achievements, and Hollmann's acknowledgment eventually allowed historians to reconstruct her contribution. The rediscovery of her story shows why scientific history must be examined carefully rather than simply accepting the names attached to famous discoveries.

Alice Ball was not famous because she had decades to build a career. She became important because, in only a few years, she solved a difficult chemical problem that had frustrated medical researchers for generations.

Her career also demonstrates the importance of education. Ball pursued two undergraduate degrees before earning a master's degree, and she entered professional research at a time when opportunities for Black women were severely restricted. Her achievements show what can happen when talent is combined with determination and access to education.

She also demonstrated the value of chemistry in medicine. Rather than simply accepting a natural substance as it existed, Ball studied its chemical structure and changed it so that it could be used more effectively. Her work was a powerful example of how laboratory chemistry could address a real human problem.

What makes Alice Ball a Hidden Legend is the combination of her scientific achievement and the shortness of her life. She became a pioneering chemist while still very young, developed a treatment that helped people suffering from a devastating disease, and then died before she could receive the recognition she deserved.

Her story is not simply about being forgotten. It is about being remembered again. Decades after her death, historians, educators, and scientists began restoring her name to the history of medicine and chemistry.

Ball's legacy reminds us that important scientific discoveries do not always come from famous laboratories or scientists with long careers. Sometimes they come from young researchers who are given an opportunity to investigate a difficult problem and refuse to stop searching for an answer.

Alice Ball's life lasted only twenty-four years, but her work reached far beyond those years. The treatment method she developed helped make chaulmoogra oil more useful against Hansen's disease and provided relief and hope to patients during a difficult period in medical history.

Her name may have disappeared from the spotlight for generations, but the science she developed could not be erased so easily. Her research continued to help patients, and eventually her story returned to the historical record.

Alice Ball proved that age, race, and gender could not determine the limits of scientific imagination. In a laboratory in Hawaii, she took a difficult medical problem and approached it through chemistry. Her solution changed treatment, even though she never lived to see how far her work would travel.

Her story deserves to be remembered because it shows that a scientist's influence can last much longer than a lifetime. Alice Ball may have worked with substances that few people today would recognize, but her determination and ingenuity helped change medical history.

She was young. She faced barriers. She was denied the recognition she deserved. But she still made a discovery that mattered. Alice Ball's story is proof that sometimes a hidden legend is someone whose greatest contribution was made quietly, in a laboratory, long before the world was ready to remember her name.
