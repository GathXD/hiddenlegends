THE HIDDEN STORY

Johan van Hulst

Born: January 28, 1911

Died: March 22, 2018

Field: Education

Known For: Rescue of Jewish Children in Amsterdam

In the middle of the Second World War, while the Netherlands was under Nazi occupation, a Dutch schoolteacher found himself facing a choice that would place innocent lives above his own safety. His name was Johan van Hulst. He was a teacher and educator, not a soldier or resistance fighter, yet his position in Amsterdam eventually placed him directly in the path of one of the darkest crimes of the war.

Van Hulst was born in Amsterdam in 1911. From an early age, education became an important part of his life. He eventually became a teacher and later worked as the director of the Reformed Teachers' Training College in Amsterdam. The school stood close to the Hollandsche Schouwburg, a theater that the Nazi authorities had turned into a holding place for Jews who were being deported.

The situation around the school became increasingly dangerous as the Nazi persecution of Dutch Jews intensified. Jewish families were separated, arrested, and transported from Amsterdam to concentration and extermination camps. Children were among those being held, and the authorities eventually began separating Jewish children from their parents.

The nursery known as the crèche stood directly across from Van Hulst's school. Jewish children were placed there before being transported away. Van Hulst soon understood what was happening. He also understood that the children were in terrible danger.

A network of Dutch resistance workers, teachers, and other helpers began finding ways to remove children from the crèche. Van Hulst became involved in this effort. His school provided an important location from which children could be moved secretly to safer places. What happened next required extraordinary courage and careful planning.

The greatest difficulty was that saving a child was not as simple as carrying the child away. Nazi guards were watching the area, and the authorities were searching for Jewish people. Every movement had to be carefully planned. A single mistake could expose the rescue network and put both the children and the rescuers in danger.

Van Hulst worked with resistance members and other people involved in the rescue operation. Children were sometimes taken from the crèche and brought through the nearby school. From there, they could be transported to families and communities willing to hide them. Some were taken away in bags, baskets, or other concealed arrangements, while others were moved through ordinary-looking journeys designed to avoid suspicion.

The rescuers faced an agonizing problem. They could not save every child. If too many children disappeared at once, the Nazis might realize what was happening. Van Hulst later described the terrible decisions involved in determining which children could be moved safely. Every child represented a life, but rescuers had to work within the limits created by surveillance and danger.

Van Hulst and the people working with him continued despite the fear. Children were transported to hiding places throughout the Netherlands, where families and resistance members took enormous risks to protect them. Many of the children survived the war because ordinary people decided that they could not simply stand by and watch.

The rescue operation was never completely safe. Van Hulst knew that discovery could mean arrest or death. He also knew that the school itself could be searched. Yet he continued using the position he had been given as an educator to help children escape persecution.

Eventually, the Nazis discovered that something was happening. The rescue network could no longer operate in the same way. By then, however, many children had already been moved out of immediate danger. Van Hulst's actions had become part of a much larger network of Dutch resistance and rescue efforts that attempted to save Jewish people throughout the occupation.

After the war, Johan van Hulst did not become famous overnight. He returned to a life centered on education and public service. He later entered politics and served in the Dutch Senate. He continued to work in education and public life for many years, carrying the memory of what had happened during the occupation.

For decades, the full significance of his wartime actions was known mainly to the people who had been involved. Many survivors remembered the people who had helped them, but Van Hulst himself often spoke modestly about his role. He did not describe himself as a hero. He understood that many people had contributed to the rescue effort and that countless others had taken risks to protect Jewish children.

Years later, survivors and historians helped bring greater attention to the rescue at the Amsterdam crèche. The children who had been saved had grown into adults with families and lives of their own. Their survival meant that generations who otherwise might never have existed were able to live.

In 2016, Van Hulst was recognized as a Righteous Among the Nations by Yad Vashem, an honor given to non-Jews who risked their lives to help Jews during the Holocaust. The recognition reflected the courage he had shown decades earlier, when helping children meant placing himself in danger.

In 2019, Van Hulst met Pope Francis at the Vatican. During their conversation, the Pope referred to the children Van Hulst had helped save. Van Hulst later recalled the painful choices that had been necessary during the rescue, including the fact that there were more children in danger than the rescuers could possibly save.

Johan van Hulst died in Amsterdam in 2018 at the age of 107. He had lived long enough to see the world change dramatically from the one he knew during the occupation. The schoolteacher who had once stood across from a building filled with frightened children had become one of the people remembered for helping some of them survive.

His story is powerful because he was not a powerful military leader or a famous politician when the most important decision of his life arrived. He was a teacher. He had classrooms, students, colleagues, and responsibilities. Yet when he saw children being targeted because of who they were, he chose to act.

Van Hulst's story also shows how resistance can take many forms. A person does not always need a weapon to resist evil. Sometimes resistance means opening a door, hiding a child, providing a safe route, or simply refusing to accept that innocent people should be treated as less than human.

The children he helped rescue could not know what dangers the adults around them were facing. For them, a journey from one building to another could have seemed ordinary. In reality, those journeys could determine whether they lived or died.

Johan van Hulst's legacy therefore lives not only in monuments, awards, or history books. It lives in the families descended from the children who survived. His life demonstrates that courage is not always loud or dramatic. Sometimes it begins with one person deciding that another person's life is worth protecting, even when doing so carries a terrible risk.
